*A sentence is one of the ways a claim can be said. It is not the claim. `kernel/gfp-claim.js` takes that literally enough to write code against it, and then goes and checks, on six real languages that do not agree with each other about where the verb goes, whether the claim underneath actually survives.*

---

## Two things named GFP, one layer apart

[Ground / Figure / Pattern](/ground-figure-pattern) already names something in this wiki: the recurring three-role structure — enabling condition, differentiated element, stabilizing integration — that EO finds *inside* each of its three independently motivated axes. That GFP is cosmological. It answers "what are the three values on the Interpretation axis shaped like."

`native/kernel/gfp-claim.js`'s GFP is a full holon-grain down from that, and it answers a narrower, more mechanical question: when a piece of code or a sentence asserts something, what is the smallest representation of *that one assertion* that survives being said in a different order? The file's own Handle credits the linguist who supplies the vocabulary directly — Talmy — not Peirce or Hegel: "A claim is a Figure placed against a Ground; the relation is the Pattern — the word order a language uses to say it is a lens, never the claim" (`README.md:360`). Both GFPs borrow the same Talmy/Langacker figure-ground pair (`ground-figure-pattern.md`, "Naming Within GFP"); the module's contribution is putting a third-slot Pattern into that inherited pair and then formalizing all three as data a computer can compare, not as a phenomenological triad. The two pages are cousins, not synonyms, and neither generates the other.

## What the three grains actually hold

The header of `native/kernel/gfp-claim.js` (lines 1–42) states the shape before any code does:

- **GROUND** (lines 46–73) — *where* the claim holds, as a holon address: `"/"`, `/p3`, `/p3/2` for prose, `/src/a.js/Foo/bar/b2` for code. `holon()` normalizes a path to its segments; `contains(a, b)` is true when `a` is `b` or an ancestor of it; `overlap(a, b)` is `contains(a,b) || contains(b,a)` — which means **siblings never meet**. `lca(a, b)` (line 64) walks the two segment lists in lockstep and stops at the first divergence, giving the smallest holon that contains both. This is not new machinery invented for claims — it is the same holon-address discipline the rest of the codebase uses for scope-respecting containment (see [Holons](/holons), "Holons in capacity ground"), reused here so that two claims are only ever compared where their addresses actually overlap.
- **FIGURE** — *what* the claim is about, keyed by **role**, never by sentence position. `gfpClaim()` (line 92) takes a `roles` object like `{ ARG0: "x", ARG1: "3" }` and freezes it after dropping empty values; `figureKey()` (line 109) turns those roles into an order-free string, sorted by role name, so "France is the capital of Paris" and "Paris is the capital of France" produce different keys even though every surface word is identical (`claimKey`, line 115, folds the relation in too). The role names are Dowty's proto-role numbering from PropBank (ARG0, ARG1, …) rather than "subject" and "object," which matters because subject/object are positional categories and this file's whole argument is that position is not where meaning lives.
- **PATTERN** — the relation itself: its label (`rel`), its polarity (`"+"`/`"-"`), and a declared `force` — `"default"` (a deeper ground's claim shadows it, like a variable rebound in a nested scope) or `"strict"` (it must hold throughout its ground, so a counterexample anywhere inside refutes it, like an invariant). Nothing about a relation is *assumed* from how it happens to be written — a caller declares whether a relation is one-valued, symmetric, or acyclic; `gfp-claim.js` itself carries no opinion. That declaration point is a real downstream consumer: `native/organs/reasoning-lint.js` (Handle: Degrees Kelsen) imports `gfpClaim`, `claimKey`, `overlap`, `lca`, and the rest of this file's ground/identity primitives directly, and uses exactly this force/polarity/ground machinery to lint a holograph for standing contradictions, one-valuedness violations, and import cycles — `native/tests/reasoning-gfp.test.js` is the proof it actually does, not just the design intent (16/16 tests pass as of this writing; re-verified directly for this article).

## The bridge, and the one thing it refuses to be a bridge for

`claimFromTriple(end1, rel, end2, opts)` (line 103) is, by its own comment, "the ONE place a triple's two ends become roles": `end1` becomes `ARG0`, `end2` becomes `ARG1`. Every reader in the codebase that produces a `{end1, label, end2}` arrangement — including the crosslingual extractor discussed below — passes through this single function to become a GFP claim. That concentration is deliberate: if role-assignment-from-position is ever wrong, there is exactly one place to fix it, not one per adapter.

What the module explicitly does *not* do is decide whether a role assignment is *correct*. `relations-gfp.js`, the extractor that produces those triples, states its own boundary in its header: "an arrangement is `{end1, label, end2}`... No subject, verb, or object anywhere" — it types a connector's cell (Ground/Figure/Pattern) from its part of speech, never writes "subject" onto a record. `gfp-claim.js` inherits that same discipline one level up: it will faithfully build `ARG0=France, ARG1=Paris` from a triple whether or not that triple is semantically right. Getting the triple right is somebody else's job (the extractor's); getting the *representation* order-invariant once you have a triple is this file's job. Confusing the two is exactly the failure mode the crosslingual experiment below turns up.

## Six orders, three code surfaces, one claim: `project` and `readBack`

WALS feature 81A (Dryer) lists six logically possible orders of subject, verb, and object; `ORDERS` (lines 122–126) names all six — SVO, SOV, VSO, VOS, OVS, OSV — as literal arrays of `"S"`/`"V"`/`"O"` slots, with `S = ARG0`, `V = rel`, `O = ARG1`. `CODE_SURFACES` (line 128) reuses the same three-letter order vocabulary for three real programming-language surfaces: infix is SVO (`x = 3`), prefix is VSO (`(= x 3)`), postfix is SOV (`x 3 =`, Forth/RPN) — the claim is that code's own syntactic variety is the same phenomenon as the world's typological variety, not a metaphor for it. A tenth lens, `"case-marked"`, drops position altogether: every token carries its role as a literal mark (`France·ARG1`) and a caller-supplied `permutation` picks any order at all, because a case-marked language's order is genuinely free.

`project(claim, lens)` (line 144) puts a claim into one of these ten lenses, producing tokens; `render` (line 157) joins them into the lens's actual surface string; `readBack(texts, lens)` (line 166) runs the inverse — takes a lens's own tokens and rebuilds the claim, reading the relation and roles off the position (or the mark, for case-marked) rather than off any stored memory of what the claim originally was. The whole point of naming it "readBack" rather than "parse" is that the lens's role assignment is treated as *the language's own eigenvalue coming back*, not a lookup against ground truth the code already possesses.

`native/tests/reasoning-gfp.test.js:10-27` is where this is actually checked, not merely asserted: for every one of the ten lenses (all four case-marked permutations included), `readBack(project(c, lens), lens)` produces a claim whose `claimKey` equals the original claim's key — a full roundtrip through every WALS order and all three code surfaces, verified, not designed-and-assumed. A second test (`reasoning-gfp.test.js:33-45`) goes further: it lints two claims that were each said through a *different* lens (one in SOV, one in VSO) and checks that `reasoning-lint.js` reaches the identical verdict — a `standing_contradiction` count of exactly 1 — no matter which of the 81 lens-pairs (9×9, excluding case-marked) the two claims arrived through. The test's own name records the motivation directly: *"the spiral's dependency claims, which the SVO default convicted three times"* — a real prior bug where treating SVO as the default order produced false contradictions that order-free GFP claims do not.

`generalizeClaims` (lines 183–222, dated 2026-09-25 in its own header) is the one place several claims sharing a relation and polarity combine into one: a role survives into the merged claim only where every input agrees on it (under a declared identity function); a role that varies keeps *every* distinct value, disclosed as `varying`, never dropped or voted on; a role some claim lacks is counted `absent`, never silently treated as agreement. `native/tests/gfp-generalize.test.js` pins this against a real, dated regression: a `-` (denied) generalization once rendered byte-identically to a `+` (asserted) one — indistinguishable on the page — until `renderGeneralization` was given the same `"denied: "` prefix `kernel/notes.js` already uses for a cut (`gfp-generalize.test.js:21-33`). It is exactly the kind of bug the module's Talmy-lens framing predicts will keep recurring: the surface is not the claim, so any code path that reconstructs a surface from a claim can quietly lose information the claim itself never lost.

Retired here as unresolved, not repeated as fact.

## The experiment: does an order-free extractor actually stay order-free across languages that disagree about order

If GFP's claim is right, an extractor built with zero language-specific word-order assumptions should be able to run, unmodified, on a VSO language, a free-order case-marked language, and an SVO language, and produce the same *kind* of result each time — because it is reading roles and relations, not positions. `native/eval/the-fold/gfp-vs-svo-crosslingual.mjs` is that test, and this article reran it directly against the treebanks present on this machine rather than trusting a previously reported number.

**Setup.** Route A ("GFP-first") is `relations-gfp.js`'s figure-connector-figure adjacency reader — order-free by construction, per its own header: "NO positional subject/verb/object extraction... NO English verb vocabulary." It runs on every language with no per-language configuration at all. Route B is a *positional* or *case-marked* reader, run only where measured infrastructure for that language already exists — never invented for the occasion: `relations-positional.js` plus a measured `RoleConfig@1` for Arabic and Hebrew, `relations-case-marked.js`'s measured Latin case prior for Latin. Where no such infrastructure exists (Ancient Greek, both Sanskrit treebanks, Naija Pidgin), the script reports "NOT AVAILABLE" rather than skipping silently. Gold is the Universal Dependencies root-VERB + nsubj/obj triple, matched on surface form (the script's own comment discloses that lemma-matching does not generalize to Arabic's vocalized gold lemmas or most of these languages' morphology — "completionRate... is reliable for every language here... `natural`/`scrambledNull` coverage+precision are... NOT yet trustworthy for Arabic/Hebrew's vocalized lemmas"). Every route is scored on the natural sentence order and on a seeded, per-sentence scrambled-token null, compared by a one-sided Fisher's exact test (`claim-null-scoring.mjs`'s `fisherGreater`, a hypergeometric tail probability computed directly from log-factorials, no external stats dependency).

**What ran, measured on this checkout, 2026-09-28.** The eval's own `LANGS` table names seven treebanks (`README.md:483-487` also states "7 treebanks"); on this machine, Naija Pidgin's treebank file is not present on disk, so the script itself printed `skip Naija / Nigerian Pidgin: treebank ud-naija_pcm not found on disk` and six languages actually ran:

| Language | Sentences | Gold predicates | GFP-first completion | GFP-first recall / precision | vs. scrambled null (Fisher p) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Arabic | 680 | 60 | 96.0% | 5.0% / 0.06% | p = 0.094 (not significant) |
| Ancient Greek | 1,047 | 138 | 84.2% | 4.3% / 0.25% | **p = 0.018 (significant)** |
| Hebrew | 491 | 73 | 92.3% | 0.0% / 0.0% | p = 1.00 |
| Latin | 2,273 | 468 | 91.6% | 1.3% / 0.11% | p = 0.248 |
| Sanskrit (UFAL) | 230 | 33 | 21.3% | 0.0% / 0.0% | p = 1.00 |
| Sanskrit (Vedic) | 2,709 | 284 | 78.7% | 0.4% / 0.02% | p = 0.726 |

Where a measured Route B existed, it told a different story on precision. Arabic's positional reader completed only 2.6% of sentences (it needs its role-config's conditions to be met, so it fires rarely) but reached 13.3% recall / 44.4% precision on the sentences it did complete, and passed the null (p = 0.024). Hebrew's positional reader completed 3.9%, reached 2.7%/10.5%, did not pass the null (p = 0.345). Latin's case-marked reader completed on zero held-out sentences (0/468) — a hard floor, not a small number, disclosed rather than rounded away.

**The honest reading.** The order-free architecture delivers exactly what it promises on the axis it was built for: *portability*. `relations-gfp.js` runs unmodified — no per-language config, no retuning — and completes a full triple on 79–96% of sentences in five of six typologically unrelated languages (only agglutinative-and-sparse Sanskrit UFAL, at 21.3%, is an outlier, plausibly a tokenization/adjacency-window artifact rather than a GFP-specific failure). That is the "ZERO ENGLISH-SHAPED ASSUMPTIONS... works on Russian, Hebrew, Korean" claim `relations-gfp.js`'s own header makes, and completion rate is the one metric in this eval immune to the lemma-matching caveat, so it is trustworthy across the board.

What it does *not* yet deliver is accuracy. Recall against gold UD triples sits at 0–5% and precision at 0.0–0.25% for every language tested — GFP-first is producing triples, but the triples it produces mostly are not the ones UD's own root-verb-plus-arguments annotation would draw. Of six languages, only Ancient Greek clears the noise floor against its own scrambled-order control (p = 0.018) — a real, if weak, signal that natural word order is doing *something* GFP-first can detect there. The other five do not, at these sample sizes and with this scoring. This is not the crosslingual GFP thesis being falsified — the thesis is about the *representation* (a claim survives its lens), not about this one adjacency-based extractor's accuracy at finding claims in the first place — but it is a genuine, disclosed negative result about the current production extractor's cross-linguistic reading quality, and English is not exempt from the same diagnosis: the reading-competency audit already on this wiki's record shows the *positional* English reader live on every session turn scoring 0.9% recall / 18.5% precision, against 74.0%/73.7% for an already-built, already-validated, but still unwired trained parser, the 74% confirmed real by the identical scrambled-null method at p = 1.9×10⁻⁴³ (`README.md`, "Reading competency audit (2026-09-23)"; [Signal from Noise](/signal-from-noise) discusses the same null-test discipline for a different signal). The pattern across both English and this six-language extension is the same: an order-free or positionally-cheap extractor completes readily; getting the *content* right against a real annotation standard is the harder, still-open problem, and the gap between "runs everywhere" and "reads correctly everywhere" is exactly where a hostile reader should look next.

## What GFP is actually a claim about

It would be easy to read `gfp-claim.js` as a claim about semantics — that this file knows what "capital of" means. It does not, and does not try to. Its claim is narrower and more defensible: that a relation between role-filled participants, once captured in a Ground/Figure/Pattern triple, can be projected into any of ten surface lenses and read back losslessly, and that the same reasoning (contradiction-checking, generalization, scope-respecting comparison) reaches the same verdict regardless of which lens produced the surface a reader happened to see. `reasoning-gfp.test.js`'s 9×9 lens-pair test is that claim, checked, not asserted. The crosslingual eval is a claim about something adjacent and harder — whether an extractor built to honor that representation can actually *find* the roles and relations worth representing, in a language it has never been tuned for — and on the evidence gathered here, the honest answer is: portably, yes; accurately, not yet, except for one weak signal in Ancient Greek. Both halves belong in the same article, because a wiki that only reported the roundtrip test would be reporting the easy half.
